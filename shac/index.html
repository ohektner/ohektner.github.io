Shac SB Index · a college basketball forecasting model · Case study, November 2026

Can ratings beat the line?

A college basketball model built only from what was known before each tip-off, tested against the betting market three ways. Each test's rules were written down and published before it ran.

The answer was no all three times. How it was tested is the part worth reading.

Test 1

Against the closing line

Model v1 is Bart Torvik's team ratings rebuilt from his archived daily files, so every prediction uses only the ratings published before that game. It weights the rating gap and home court from earlier seasons. Four extra inputs (early-season form, rest, offense versus defense, conference home court) were tried and none earned a place.

It met its first goal. Its distance to the closing line is a hair smaller than Torvik's own number (1.989 versus 2.006 points per game). Against the spread it went 50.3%, below the 52.4% needed to beat the standard −110 price. Calibrated, its chance of covering barely moves off 50%. A Lean would need about 20 points of disagreement with the line, which never happens.

This test also caught a data problem. The first run showed a 10–1 record on the strongest calls. Almost all of it came from historical lines with the right number on the wrong team, such as Duke −8.5 stored as Texas Tech −8.5. Two checks now drop those lines, and a hand check of six against public listings confirmed five, with the sixth likely.

Test 2

Against the night-before line

Professional bettors don't wait for the closing line. They take an early number and judge themselves by whether the line then moves their way. So the next question was whether v1 sees where early lines are heading.

A first read on one week of this season's games looked strong: +0.46 points of CLV. A control added afterwards showed why. That week, lines drifted toward underdogs, and "always take the underdog" earned +0.33. The model leans to underdogs, so most of its result was the drift.

The real test used a bought month of historical odds: 1,761 timed snapshots across four seasons, three per game day. Before any result was computed, two checks ran:

  • The archive's closing lines matched the separate historical source to a median of 0.25 points. Passed.
  • At least 90% of games needed a line by 10:20 p.m. the night before. Two seasons fell short, at 82.0% and 89.7%, because about 8% of lines post only on game day. The decision to go ahead was recorded before any result existed.

The result was +0.015 points per game, with a 95% interval that includes zero. The curve for turning edges into calls came out slightly negative, so there would be no calls at all.

Test 3

With perfect knowledge of who sits

The planned AI layer was meant to read injury and lineup news and act before the line moves. Before building it, a ceiling test asked a harder question: if we had known exactly which rotation players would miss each game, would the adjusted model beat the night-before line? Hindsight was used on purpose, so the real system could only do worse.

The adjustment followed the layer's approved spec. Each missing player was weighted by minutes played, and one learned value turned that into points. That value came out at about one point per full-time player, a real effect but a small one.

Method

How the tests were kept honest

  • Point in timeEach prediction uses the ratings file that existed before tip-off. Pinning down when each daily file became public caught a rule that would have leaked results from 52 late-night games.
  • Rules firstEvery test's method and pass/fail reading was committed to the public repo before it ran. The timestamps are in the pull request history.
  • Checks firstData checks run before any result. When one failed, the decision on what to do was written down before looking.
  • ControlsBeating zero isn't enough. Each result is compared with a coin flip and with simple rules like "always home".
  • Bad data outLines from one provider failed a closing-line test and were dropped. Lines on the wrong team were caught by the fake 10–1 record and removed.

What it means

Public ratings are already in the price

By the night before, the market already reflects what public team ratings know, and a minutes-based read of who is sitting adds nothing measurable. That matches the common view that you can't beat the market with public numbers, and here it is measured, with each rule fixed before the result.

Edges, where they exist, come from information and speed. These were not tested:

  • Valuing players individually, so a missing star counts for more than a missing role player.
  • Slow books that lag a sharp book's move. Our snapshots, seven a day, are likely too coarse.
  • Softer markets: totals, first halves, player props.
  • The very first numbers posted, at one or two books. A hint (+0.10 points on 666 games) isn't conclusive.
